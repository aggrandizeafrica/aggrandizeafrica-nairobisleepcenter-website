Insomnia: sleep again, without depending on pills

Insomnia is the most common reason people come to our centre. It is real, it is exhausting, and it responds well to the right treatment.

Book an appointmentWhatsApp us

What counts as insomnia?

Insomnia means having trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early and being unable to get back to sleep, even though you have the chance to sleep, and feeling the effects during the day. A few bad nights during a stressful week is normal. When it happens at least three nights a week for three months or more, it is called chronic insomnia.

Common signs

  • Taking more than half an hour to fall asleep
  • Waking in the night and lying awake for long periods
  • Waking much earlier than you want to
  • Daytime tiredness, poor concentration, irritability or low mood
  • Worrying about sleep, and dreading bedtime

What causes it?

Insomnia often starts with a trigger such as stress, bereavement, illness, shift work, a new baby or travel, and then carries on because of habits and worry that build up around sleep. It can also be linked to anxiety and depression, pain, menopause, some medicines, caffeine and alcohol, or another sleep disorder such as sleep apnoea or restless legs. Finding these contributors is part of your assessment.

How we help

  • A full sleep assessment covering your sleep pattern, health, medicines and mood. You may be asked to keep a sleep diary for one to two weeks.
  • Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), the recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia. It is a structured programme with our psychology team that resets your sleep pattern and the thoughts that keep you awake.
  • Psychology and psychiatry support where anxiety, depression or trauma are part of the picture.
  • Careful use of medicines where appropriate, usually for the short term and alongside CBT-I rather than instead of it.
  • A sleep study if we suspect another sleep disorder is breaking up your sleep.

Tonight: keep a fixed wake-up time, even after a bad night; get out of bed if you have been awake for a long time and return when sleepy; and keep caffeine to the morning. These habits support treatment, but persistent insomnia deserves a proper assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a sleep study for insomnia?

Not always. Insomnia is usually diagnosed from your history and a sleep diary. We recommend a sleep study when another condition, such as sleep apnoea or limb movements, may be disturbing your sleep.

How long does CBT-I take?

Most programmes run over about four to eight sessions. Many people notice their sleep improving within the first few weeks.

Are sleeping pills a good long-term answer?

For most people, no. They can help in the short term, but CBT-I gives longer-lasting results without the risk of dependence.
