Obstructive sleep apnoea: diagnosis and treatment

Sleep apnoea is one of the most common sleep disorders we see, and one of the most treatable once it has been properly measured.

Book an appointmentWhatsApp us

What is obstructive sleep apnoea?

In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) the soft tissues at the back of the throat relax and narrow the airway while you sleep. Breathing becomes shallow (a hypopnoea) or stops altogether for ten seconds or more (an apnoea). Each time, oxygen levels dip and the brain briefly wakes you to restart breathing. Most people never remember these arousals, but they can happen dozens of times an hour, all night.

Symptoms

  • Loud, habitual snoring, often with pauses, snorts or gasps
  • A partner noticing that you stop breathing
  • Waking unrefreshed, however long you slept
  • Falling asleep in meetings, in traffic or in front of the TV
  • Morning headaches, a dry mouth or sore throat
  • Waking at night to pass urine
  • Poor concentration, irritability or low mood

Who is at risk?

OSA is more common in men, after middle age, and in people carrying extra weight around the neck and middle, but slim and young people get it too. Large tonsils, a narrow jaw, nasal blockage, alcohol or sedatives at night and a family history all raise the risk. In children, enlarged tonsils and adenoids are the usual cause.

Why it matters. Untreated sleep apnoea is linked to high blood pressure, heart rhythm problems, heart attack, stroke and type 2 diabetes, and drowsy driving is a serious road risk. If you are falling asleep at the wheel, please do not drive until you have been assessed.

How we diagnose it

The gold-standard test is an overnight sleep study (polysomnography) in our sleep laboratory. Sensors record your breathing, oxygen levels, heart rhythm, brain activity and body position while you sleep, and a sleep technician watches over you all night. The result is your apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI): the number of breathing events per hour of sleep.

AHI (events per hour)Severity in adults
Fewer than 5Within the normal range
5 to 14Mild
15 to 29Moderate
30 or moreSevere

Treatment

Your treatment plan depends on the severity and the cause. It may include:

  • CPAP therapy, the first-line treatment for moderate and severe OSA. A quiet machine delivers gentle air pressure through a mask to hold the airway open. We help you choose the right machine and mask, and follow up until it feels comfortable.
  • Airway and ENT treatment where a blockage is the cause, such as nasal obstruction or enlarged tonsils, with our colleagues at Nairobi ENT Clinic.
  • Positional therapy if your apnoea happens mainly when you sleep on your back.
  • Weight management, reducing alcohol near bedtime, and reviewing sedating medicines.
  • Oral appliances that bring the lower jaw forward, for some people with mild to moderate OSA.

Frequently asked questions

Is snoring always sleep apnoea?

No. Many people snore without apnoea. But snoring with pauses, gasping or daytime sleepiness should be checked with a sleep study.

Will I have to use CPAP forever?

CPAP controls sleep apnoea rather than curing it, so most people use it long term. If the cause changes, for example after significant weight loss or airway surgery, a repeat sleep study can show whether you still need it.

Can children have sleep apnoea?

Yes. Children who snore most nights, breathe through their mouth or sleep restlessly should be assessed. Enlarged tonsils and adenoids are a common, treatable cause.
